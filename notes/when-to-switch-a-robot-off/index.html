When should you switch a robot off?

Your robot has had a few bad weeks. Is it broken, or is it a bad patch? We took 32 real robots, let them trade the months their backtests never saw, and checked which way of answering gets it right.

The question

You have a robot running. It has a backtest that looked good. It has had a few bad weeks. Is it broken, or is it just a bad patch? Switch it off too early and you kill a good robot on its unlucky streak. Too late, and you pay for the lesson.

Most tools answer by looking at the win rate: if the robot still wins as often as its backtest said, it is “fine”. We used to do the same. Then we checked that answer against robots whose fate we already knew.

The experiment

32 robots built with StrategyQuant X, each with its backtest: what it promised. We ran every one of them in MetaTrader 5's Strategy Tester over the months after its backtest ended, months it had never seen. Then we looked at what really happened:

  • 21 lost money: they won less than $0.95 for every $1 they lost.
  • 5 were roughly flat.
  • 6 made money.

So the right answer is known for every robot. Now: what does each way of judging say?

Five robots that were losing money were declared “still like their backtest”. One of them had lost $20,967 in 94 trades: it was still winning often enough to pass. And three robots that were making money were declared “gone”, because they won less often than promised, though they were making money. One of them was $3,763 up.

A robot can win as often as it promised and lose money. It can win less often and make money. The win rate is a detail; the money is the point.

Judging by the money

So we changed the question: is it making less money per dollar risked than it promised, beyond what bad luck explains? In numbers: its profit factor (dollars won for each dollar lost) against the one in its backtest, with a margin for luck, and only once it has 20 trades to judge.

With a 90% margin, twelve of the 21 are caught, and not one good robot is switched off by mistake. And it never says “holding up” about a robot that is losing money.

What about the other nine? Most had too few trades for anyone to tell bad luck from a broken robot, and for those the honest answer is “too early”. A rule that caught all 21 would also kill good robots in their bad weeks: judging by “losing money, full stop” catches 15 instead of 12, and switches off two flat robots by mistake.

What 32 robots can and cannot say

32 robots is not a big sample, and most of them had between 20 and 50 trades in their new months. We compared four rules on them and kept the one with no false alarms, so take the exact numbers as a first measurement, not a law.

If you have a robot running, the question is not whether it had a bad week. It is whether the money says it has stopped being the robot you bought.
