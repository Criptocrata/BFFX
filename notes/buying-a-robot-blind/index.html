Buying a robot blind

Someone wants to sell you a trading robot, and its backtest says $8,742 of profit. We opened the box with BFInspector, the way a buyer would: the code, the report, and the costs nobody mentions.

The box

The seller sends three files: the robot itself (an .mq5 for MetaTrader 5), a PDF with its backtest, and a .sqx, the StrategyQuant X project it came from. The PDF looks good: $8,742 of profit over 1,211 trades on the S&P 500, from 2012 to 2026, a profit factor of 1.24 (it wins $1.24 for every $1 it loses) and a worst fall of 10.7%.

We opened the box with BFInspector, the free tool we give away, the way a buyer would. This is a real StrategyQuant X robot, not one made up for the occasion. Here is what came out.

What the robot really does

BFInspector reads the code without running it and writes down, in plain words, when it buys, when it sells and how much it risks.

  • It only buys. Its selling rule asks for two prices calculated in different ways to be exactly equal. That practically never happens. All 1,211 trades in its own report are buys.
  • One of its two buying conditions is decoration. It is true almost all the time, so it filters nothing.
  • What does the work: a buy order above yesterday's high, a stop at 0.3% of the price, a target at 1.1%, a trailing stop, and everything closed on Friday evening. It risks 1% of the balance per trade.
  • None of the seven known traps (doubling up after a loss, trading without a stop, looking into the future, account locks…) is in the code.

None of this is in the PDF. A buyer who only read the PDF would not know they were buying a robot that never sells.

The PDF has no trades

The PDF is a summary: totals, a monthly table, charts. Not a single trade. You cannot check a summary against anything. BFInspector says so and asks you for the .sqx, which carries every trade.

Do its numbers add up? Yes

With the .sqx, its 1,211 trades add up to exactly the $8,742.72 its report declares. Nobody edited the summary. That is the first thing a dishonest report gets wrong, and this one is honest.

The spread it never paid

Here is the catch. The .sqx also says what it cost to trade during the test: a spread of 0.03 pips. Its own instrument sheet, inside the same file, says the S&P 500 normally costs 4.6 pips. The test paid a spread 153 times thinner than real life.

BFInspector replays the same 1,211 trades at the spread its own sheet calls typical. $8,742 becomes $5,976: almost a third of the profit was never going to reach anybody's account. It still makes money, but it is not the robot in the PDF.

Your broker's candles are not its candles (and that is not fraud)

A good check is to put its trades on top of your own price history: a trade at a price the market never touched is invented. We used two years of the S&P 500 from a regular retail broker. 84 of 355 prices fell outside their candle.

It looks damning, and it isn't. 83 of the 84 fell below the candle, entries and exits alike, by a little. Its test used another data source, which quotes the same index a few points lower, and at night the two can drift well apart. An invented price falls on the side that suits the seller; 60 of these work against the seller. Nobody invents a price that makes them lose.

The first time we ran this, BFInspector called it fraud. Version 2.5.0 recognizes the mark of another source, warns instead of accusing, and asks for candles from the same source as the test.

On the broker's own prices

So we did what that check asks for, the other way round: we ran the robot on the broker's prices. The last year of its backtest, June 2025 to June 2026, in that broker's MetaTrader 5 Strategy Tester: 87 trades. Same months, different prices. Against its backtest it trades at the same pace (1.7 trades a week against 1.6), wins as often (35.6% against 35.8%) and makes a little less per trade ($0.96 per lot against $1.44), well inside what its own history allows: 418 of a thousand random samples of its backtest did worse. On the broker's prices it is still the same robot.

And after its backtest ended?

That is the one question no box can answer. From 9 June to 16 October 2026, months its backtest never saw, the same Strategy Tester gave it 26 trades. It won 31% of them, and it lost money: $0.78 for every $1 it lost, against the $1.24 it promised.

Twenty-six trades are too few to tell a bad patch from a broken robot. BFQuarantine, our program for watching a robot after you buy it, says exactly that: losing money, but not yet clearly worse than promised. How it decides when to switch a robot off is a note of its own.

What the box told us

  • What it does: a buy-only robot on the S&P 500, with one condition doing nothing.
  • Whether the report is honest: yes, its totals match its trades, and on the broker's prices it behaves the same.
  • What it is worth to you: about a third less than it says, because its test never paid a real spread.

None of this told us whether it would make money after its backtest. Its first months say not so far, and they are still too few to be sure. That is what quarantine is for. But you would not pay for a car without opening the bonnet, and this took five minutes.
